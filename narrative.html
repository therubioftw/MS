Prototype Approach

Building a product for manufacturers

How I would start, what I would learn first, and who wins what.
The prototype is here — start on the Workflow screen, which shows where its four screens sit in the manufacturer's day and what is deliberately not built.

Where I landed. Manufacturers win accounts on speed-to-quote. They cannot quote fast because the price is not available at the moment of quoting, and because anything non-standard stops and waits for a person. Both of those are visible in the documents you sent, not inferred.

Materialspace already sits on one side of that exchange. The manufacturer product is the other half of a loop you own half of. And the first move is not to build catalog ingestion — you have that — but to hand the manufacturer authority over data you currently infer, in exchange for the one thing you cannot infer at any accuracy: what it costs.

01What the manufacturer is trying to get

Four goals, in the order I think they would rank them. Each screen exists to serve one of these, and where a goal is only partly served I have said so.

1 · Return a price before the other three manufacturers quoting the same job

This is the whole game. The products are undifferentiated, so the account goes to whoever answers first and answers well. Everything else in this document is downstream of it.

Served by: the Inbox, which sorts by how long a dealer has been waiting rather than by when it arrived. And the Quote screen, where eleven of sixteen lines price without anyone touching them, and a single hard line can go out as an allowance instead of holding the other fifteen hostage.

2 · Be the manufacturer that is easy to deal with

The second half of the anchor, and the one I would argue is underrated. When the product is a commodity, the relationship is the product.

Served by: queries going back alongside the price rather than instead of it. Catching the two-finish violation before it becomes a rejection two days later. A committed lead time rather than “subject to capacity.”

Only partly. Most of what a dealer means by service happens after the order is placed — was it confirmed, did anything change, when does it ship. That stretch is on the workflow map and it is not built. The twenty-four hour sign-off window in their own p.71 process is the first thing I would add, because it is the first moment after the quote where a dealer feels either served or ignored.

3 · Know what is coming before it arrives

Their words: they hear nothing for a year, then a massive order lands and the factory cannot absorb it.

Served by: Pipeline, in aggregate, with no dealer named — because a factory schedule needs totals, not names.

4 · Have a catalog that is correct and current

Served by: the Catalog change feed, where the manufacturer publishes changes rather than replacing a document, and each change carries a permanence and a scope.

What is deliberately not a goal here

Chasing deals. Selling more to the dealers they already have. Replacing their ERP. Each is a reasonable thing for a manufacturer to want, and each would make this a different, worse product.

What speed is worth — the shape of the arithmetic

Nobody has given me a real number, so this is a worked hypothetical, and every input is invented. The point is the shape, and the first thing I would do is replace each of these with something real.

  • Say a manufacturer quotes 400 projects a year, averaging $180k.
  • Say returning a price same-day instead of in six days moves win rate by two points.
  • That is eight more projects, or about $1.4M of revenue, against no additional selling cost.

Two points is the number I would want to argue about, because everything hangs on it. It is also answerable — a manufacturer knows their win rate, and they know roughly how often they were late.

02What I would learn before building

From your own data first — days, no access required

Three questions can be answered from what Materialspace already holds, before anyone takes a meeting. I would not start interviews until these are done, because they change who I would talk to.

  • Does estimate activity predict award? The pipeline screen rests entirely on the idea that a dealer revising an estimate means the deal is still alive. Backtest it against won/lost outcomes already in your dealer data. If revision activity does not predict award, that screen is wrong and I want to know in week one.
  • Which manufacturer overlaps most with your existing dealers? The beachhead should be a query, not a judgment call. Whoever the most of your dealers already quote against is the one whose onboarding immediately makes the platform better for customers you already have.
  • How uncertain is what you already hold? You have derived catalog data nobody has ever confirmed. Measuring how much of it is low-confidence tells you the size of the ask you are about to make.

From dealers — five to eight conversations

Walk me through the last quote that came back wrong. Where exactly did it break?

Do you tell manufacturers they are competing on a job?

What would you need in return to let a manufacturer see that a job is live?

Would you rather have a firm price in two hours with one line as an allowance, or a complete price in three days?

That last one is the decisive question. The entire brief rests on speed being what wins. If dealers answer "three days," the thesis is weaker than stated and the product should be aimed somewhere else.

From manufacturers — three to five, including the one you are courting

How many hands touch a quote today, and what is your real median time to return one?

Where does the price book live, and how are per-dealer multipliers stored?

What share of inbound lines are non-standard?

How far ahead must you commit materials, and what does a surprise order actually cost you?

What would make me stop

If manufacturers will not connect a price book and multiplier structure, there is no product — not a smaller product, none. Everything downstream assumes the ability to price.

If dealers say a complete price in three days beats a firm price in two hours, the speed anchor is wrong and the exercise should be re-framed before anything gets built.

03Breaking it into steps

Phase 0 — Show them their own catalog~3 weeks

Take what Materialspace already holds on one manufacturer and present it back to them. No upload, no data entry, no new ingestion. They confirm the taxonomy, connect the price book and multiplier structure, and start publishing changes.

This is a demo, not a request — which is what makes it a plausible first ask of a large manufacturer. It also converts inferred data into confirmed data, which is the half of "catalog completeness and accuracy" that no amount of parsing can reach.

Kill criterion: no price book connected by week three means no product. Find out then.
Phase 1 — Quote inbox and exception resolution~9 weeks

One manufacturer, every inbound channel — structured requests from Materialspace dealers and scanned markups from everyone else. This is the value anchor and the bulk of the build.

Metric: median time to return a price, target same day. Secondary: share of lines priced without a human touching them.

Phase 2 — Aggregate demand~6 weeks

Forward load by period, across all dealers, with no attribution. Only worth building once enough quote volume runs through that manufacturer for the forecast to mean anything.

Metric: forecast accuracy against orders that actually land.

Phase 3 — Attributed pipeline, opt-inlater

Named projects, dealer by dealer, only where that dealer has opted in and only in exchange for something concrete. Deliberately last, because it is the one piece that can cost you dealer trust.

Explicitly not now

Monetization. Self-serve onboarding. A second manufacturer. Anything aimed at the manufacturer's sales team rather than their factory. Each of these is a reasonable thing to want and a bad thing to do before Phase 1 has a number attached to it.

04Where I would start, and why

Phase 0, with one manufacturer, on the messy lane.

  • It gates everything. Nothing prices without a price book and a multiplier. Every screen in the prototype is downstream of that one connection.
  • It is your own stated reason number one — and specifically the accuracy half of it, which is structurally unavailable without the manufacturer.
  • Only they can authorize it. Which makes it the natural first ask, and turns the manufacturer into a participant rather than a subject.
  • It is the cheapest thing to be wrong about. Three weeks, no UI to speak of, and a real answer at the end.
Messy lane before clean lane

The obvious objection to the prototype is that Materialspace generates the estimate, so why build an AI to read a document someone printed and scribbled on.

Because today almost none of a manufacturer's hundreds of dealers are on Materialspace. On day one the value is handling all their inbound, whatever form it arrives in. Then the manufacturer starts telling dealers "send it through Materialspace and you get same-day pricing" — and that is the distribution engine in reason number two. The messy lane is what buys you the clean lane.

05Who wins what

SideWinsGives up / risks
Dealer Prices back same day instead of in a week. Fewer rejected orders. A committed lead time instead of “subject to capacity.” One place to work rather than a mailbox. Pipeline exposure — and their multiplier is negotiated on volume, so a visible forward book costs them negotiating asymmetry. Longer term, fear that the manufacturer goes direct.
Manufacturer Win rate, via the only thing that differentiates a commodity — speed and service. Lower cost per quote. Forward load they can actually plan a factory against. Their discount ladder becomes visible to a third party. Dependence on that third party for the customer relationship. Ownership of catalog upkeep they currently get for free.
Materialspace Catalog data that is confirmed rather than inferred. Both sides of the transaction. A reason for manufacturers to push their own dealers onto the platform. An enterprise line to sell into. Becomes the accountable party when a price is wrong. Sits between two parties whose interests genuinely diverge, and has to keep choosing.
The conflict, and how the design answers it

A factory schedule needs to know how many units land in week 47. It does not need to know whose job they are. Attribution is what makes dealers refuse, and planning does not require it.

So aggregate demand is the default and names nobody, attribution is a separate opt-in layer, and un-opted projects still count toward the totals while staying masked. Planning ships. Sales waits.

The same boundary runs the other way: dealer-specific overlays — exclusive sizes, approved modifications, negotiated tiers — are confidential between the manufacturer and that dealer, and nothing in the aggregate views is derived from them.

06What could go wrong

Product

Parse errors are silent. W1818 and W1858 are both plausible SKUs, so a wrong reading does not announce itself — it announces itself as a wrong cabinet in a hundred-unit building, months later. This is why the prototype shows confidence and refuses to recommend below a threshold, and why an audit trail from price back to source line matters more than accuracy percentage.

The confidence threshold is an economic question nobody has answered. It should be set from the cost of a wrong auto-accept against the cost of asking, per line type — not picked by whoever tunes the model.

Nothing in the prototype learns. Every correction a quoting desk makes is a labelled example of exactly the thing that was hard, and right now it evaporates. The difference between a demo and a system worth paying for is that the second time a dealer writes “F-L,” nobody has to be asked again — and that once one manufacturer has confirmed what an underscore means in their book, it is confirmed. I would build the correction loop in Phase 1, not after, because the volume of corrections is highest at the start and that is exactly when they are being thrown away.

Process

The price book may not exist in any machine-readable form. The specifications guide is a print document. There is no reason to assume the price book is better, and some reason to assume it is a spreadsheet with a decade of local conventions in it.

The quoting desk is one or two people who have done it their way for twenty years, and whose value to their employer is partly that they are the only ones who know how. A tool that makes their job legible is not automatically welcome.

Market

Dealers may read this as the first step to disintermediation — and they are your paying customers today. Getting the sequencing wrong here does not slow the manufacturer product down, it damages the business that exists.

Manufacturer ordering portals already exist. The differentiator is not the portal. It is that the dealer network is already there and the system takes an order in whatever form it turns up.

07Assumptions I took

  • The dollar figures on the sample order document are line totals, not per-unit prices.
  • The handwritten “F-L” means finished-left, and “b” means “by”.
  • Revenue being fourth in your list of four reasons is deliberate — so early on this is cheap or free to the manufacturer, and monetization follows rather than leads.
  • Materialspace already ingests manufacturer catalogs for the dealer side, so catalog parsing is an existing capability rather than something to build.
  • Dealer names, volumes, and every figure on the catalog screen are invented. The line items, the markup, the code grammar, and every page citation are not.
  • The win-rate arithmetic in section 01 is a worked hypothetical with invented inputs. It is there to show the shape of the case, not to claim the number.
  • The roles on each screen — quoting desk, plant scheduling, sales operations — are my guess at how a manufacturer of this size divides the work. At a smaller one it may be the same two people wearing all three hats, which would change who the product is designed for.
