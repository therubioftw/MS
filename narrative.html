Prototype Approach

Building a product for manufacturers

A manufacturer answers every quote request the same day, whatever form it arrives in.

That's the product. The rest of this page covers what makes it possible, what it could lead to, and what I'd leave out for now.

This is the written version of the presentation: how I'd start, what I'd want to learn first, and who wins what. The prototype is here.

Where I landed. Manufacturers win accounts by getting a price back fast. Two things slow them down. The price usually isn't available while the quote is being put together, because it depends on a separate price book and a discount agreed with each dealer. And anything that isn't a standard catalog item has to wait for someone at the manufacturer to check and price it.

Materialspace already works with the dealers who send those requests. A manufacturer product covers the other side of the same exchange. The first step isn't building catalog reading, which Materialspace already does. It's getting the manufacturer to take responsibility for their own catalog data, and to connect their pricing.

The assumption everything rests on

Everything here assumes that quoting faster wins more jobs, by enough to matter to a manufacturer. The brief says so, and it's the first thing I'd check.

If manufacturers already quote within a couple of days, and dealers really choose on price or long relationships, then same-day quoting won't win much. The product would still be worth building, but it would be sold differently.

How I'd test it. Ask dealers, since they're the ones choosing. On your last few jobs, why did you go with that manufacturer? Would you rather have a firm price in two hours with one line still open, or a complete price in three days? I'd ask dealers rather than manufacturers, because manufacturers rarely find out why they lost a job.

If speed isn't the reason. The same tool can be sold as a way to save work instead of a way to win jobs. Today someone at the manufacturer retypes and checks orders by hand. If the tool does most of that, the same team can handle more quotes without hiring. That's a pitch to whoever runs operations, not to the head of sales. Materialspace already makes this argument to dealers: a customer on your website describes doing with one estimator what used to take four. In that case I'd move the planning work earlier, because it becomes the bigger benefit. The catalog and pricing work stays first either way, because the rest depends on it.

01What the manufacturer is trying to get

Four goals, in the order I think a manufacturer would rank them. For each one I've noted which part of the prototype serves it, and where it only partly does.

1 · Get a price back before the other manufacturers quoting the same job

The products are largely interchangeable, so the job tends to go to whoever answers first and gets it right.

In the prototype: the Inbox sorts requests by how long the dealer has been waiting. On the Quote screen, eleven of sixteen lines are priced automatically. A difficult custom line can go out with an estimated price, confirmed later, so it doesn't hold up the rest of the quote.

2 · Be the manufacturer that's easy to work with

When the products are interchangeable, service is a big part of what the dealer is choosing.

In the prototype: questions for the dealer go out together with the price instead of delaying it. An order that mixes two finishes is flagged before it's sent, because Smart's rules allow one finish per order and it would otherwise be rejected later.

Only partly covered. A lot of what dealers experience as service happens after they place the order: was it entered correctly, did anything change, when will it ship. I haven't built that part. The first thing I'd add is the review step right after an order is entered. Smart's order process (spec book, page 71) gives the dealer 24 hours to check the confirmation and ask for changes before the order is locked into production. It's the first moment after the quote where a dealer can feel well looked after, or not.

3 · Know what orders are coming before they arrive

In the brief, the manufacturer describes pricing a project, hearing nothing for up to a year, and then receiving a huge order the factory struggles to absorb.

In the prototype: the Pipeline screen shows expected volume by week against factory capacity. Whether it should also show dealer activity on individual projects is an open question, covered in section 07.

4 · Keep the catalog accurate, with the manufacturer in charge of it

As I understand it, Materialspace already reads manufacturer catalogs for its dealers, and checks and cleans up that data itself, without anyone at the manufacturer confirming it. With the manufacturer on the platform, they review and correct their own data, and bring in catalogs Materialspace doesn't have yet. Materialspace still does the technical work behind the scenes.

In the prototype: the Catalog screen shows how codes are read, where they're ambiguous, and what dealers ask for that isn't in the catalog. It also shows a list of changes for the manufacturer to publish. I don't know how often catalogs or prices actually change. They may stay the same for years. That's a question for manufacturers (section 03).

Not in scope for now

These are all things a manufacturer might reasonably want, and some may make sense later. I'd leave them out at the start, to focus on where the pain is biggest and where Materialspace can offer something new.

  • Helping manufacturers chase deals, by showing their sales teams which dealers are working on what. It depends on dealers being comfortable sharing, which is still open.
  • Selling more to existing dealers, through upselling or promotions. Useful, but it doesn't make quoting faster.
  • Replacing the manufacturer's order or accounting system. That's a big, risky switch for them. The product should work alongside what they already use.
  • Charging manufacturers early. Your list of reasons puts revenue last, so I'd focus first on getting manufacturers and their catalogs onto the platform. Later, it could be charged per quote, as Parspec does, or as a subscription per manufacturer.
  • Self-serve sign-up, and a second manufacturer. Both should wait until the first manufacturer shows the product works.

What faster quoting could be worth

I don't have real numbers, so this is a rough example to show how I'd think about it. I'd like your view on whether it's realistic.

  • A manufacturer prices 400 projects a year, averaging $180,000 each.
  • Answering the same day instead of in six days means winning 2 more out of every 100 projects priced.
  • That's 8 extra projects a year, or about $1.4 million in revenue.

Everything depends on that "2 more out of 100", and it's hard to measure. Manufacturers often don't know why they lost a job, while dealers do, which is why I'd ask dealers. Is that a reasonable guess, or does speed decide more jobs than that, or fewer?

02How a manufacturer's day works

The prototype covers a small part of a longer process. Roughly, different people use it at different rhythms:

  • Every day: the quoting team goes through new requests and prices them. They're the main users.
  • Weekly: sales operations update catalog and price information.
  • Monthly: plant scheduling reviews what's coming.

Counting the quoting steps and Smart's order process, it takes about sixteen steps to get from a dealer's request to delivery. The prototype covers the quoting steps: receiving a request, reading it, resolving problems, and sending the price. It partly covers planning. It doesn't cover anything after the order is placed, such as order entry, the 24-hour review, production, delivery or claims. I left those out on purpose, because jobs are won or lost at the quoting stage.

These roles are my guess for a large manufacturer. At a smaller one, the same two people might do all of it, which would change who the product is designed for.

03What I'd want to learn before building

For each question, I've noted what the different answers would change. If I could only ask four, they'd be the ones marked Ask first.

From Materialspace's own data

Does Materialspace know which quoted jobs its dealers actually won?

If yes: check whether activity on an estimate, such as recent changes, predicts that the job gets won. If it does, the pipeline view can rely on it.

If no: that can't be checked from data. The signal would have to come from asking dealers, or be dropped.

Which manufacturer do the most Materialspace dealers already buy from?

That's the natural first manufacturer, because bringing them on helps customers Materialspace already has.

How complete and reliable is the catalog data Materialspace already holds?

Mostly reliable: the first ask of the manufacturer is small, a review rather than a fix.

Many gaps or errors: the first step is a bigger cleanup, and the manufacturer needs a clearer reason to take it on.

Do Materialspace's dealers design in 2020, and does Materialspace use 2020 catalogs?

If yes: 2020 is something the business relies on as well as a competitor, so moving away from it needs to be gradual.

If no: 2020 is simply the competitor to replace.

From dealers

On your last few jobs, why did you choose that manufacturer?Ask first

Mostly speed and responsiveness: the main assumption holds.

Mostly price or relationship: sell it as saving work rather than winning jobs, as described at the top.

How many manufacturers do you usually ask to price a job?

Several at once: it's a race, so speed matters most.

One preferred, others as backup: the relationship matters more, and speed less.

Would you rather have a firm price in two hours with one line still open, or a complete price in three days?

Two hours: sending prices with some lines estimated is worth building.

Three days: complete prices matter more, so accuracy comes before speed.

Would you mind if a manufacturer could see whether a job you asked them to price is still active?

Don't mind: the pipeline can show dealer activity on every project, which is simpler and more useful to the manufacturer.

Do mind: show activity only as weekly totals, and on a single project only when the dealer asks for something in return (section 07).

Walk me through the last quote that came back wrong. Where did it go wrong?

This shows which mistakes matter most, and where the Quote screen's checks should focus.

From manufacturers

Who prepares your quotes: your own team, or independent sales reps?Ask first

Own team: the manufacturer is the user, as in the prototype.

Reps: reps may be the right users, or at least important for getting dealers on board. In electrical products, Parspec built its product for reps (section 06).

Would you ask your dealers to send quote requests through a particular platform?Ask first

Yes: the dealer growth described in section 04 is realistic.

No, they'd rather not push dealers: growth depends on Materialspace winning dealers itself, and the manufacturer product is mainly something to sell, not a way to grow.

How long does a quote take today, usually and at worst, and how many people touch it?

This is the starting point for measuring the product.

Where does your price book live, and would you connect it?Ask first

Yes: the full product is possible.

No: a smaller product is still possible (see the box below).

How often do your catalog and prices change?

Rarely: it's set up once, with occasional updates, and maintenance is a small part of the product.

Often: keeping it current is real ongoing work, so having the manufacturer run it matters more.

How do you schedule production: by total volume, or around rush jobs and priority dealers?

By volume: weekly totals are enough for planning, and dealer names aren't needed.

Around urgency and priority: totals hide what matters, so planning needs detail per job, which brings the dealer question back.

How far ahead do you need to know about an order, and what does a surprise order cost you?

This shows how valuable the planning view is, and how far ahead it needs to look. Smart buys materials 12 to 14 business days before building (page 71).

Would you be willing to do setup work this early?

Yes: start with the catalog and the price connection.

Not yet: Materialspace does more of the work up front, and only asks the manufacturer to review.

What would make you uncomfortable relying on a platform your competitors also use?

This shapes the commitments described in section 07: who owns the data, whose name is on the quote, and staying neutral between manufacturers.

If manufacturers won't connect their prices

I first thought there'd be no product without prices. There's still a smaller one. Materialspace can turn a messy, marked-up order into a clean, checked list of catalog items, flagging problems such as non-standard sizes and mixed finishes. The manufacturer then prices it in their own system.

That still removes most of the retyping, which is the first pain point in the brief. It doesn't give same-day prices, so it falls short of the main goal, but it's a real starting point.

04How I'd break it into steps

I haven't put timelines on these. I'd estimate them with the engineering team, once we know how requests will reach the product and what it has to connect to.

Phase 0 · Show the manufacturer what Materialspace already knows

Start with one manufacturer. Show them their catalog as Materialspace already holds it, including the codes it isn't sure about. Ask them to take over reviewing and correcting it, and to connect their prices.

Two risks: they may not want to do any work this early, and there may not be much to maintain if their catalog rarely changes. That's why the first ask is a review rather than data entry, and why Materialspace does the heavy lifting.

Next step once prices are connected, or once we decide to start with the smaller product.
Phase 1 · Quote requests and problem lines

One manufacturer, taking requests in whatever form they arrive. This is most of the work, and where the main value is.

How requests get in at first: a dedicated email address. The manufacturer forwards quote requests to it, or asks dealers to send them there directly, and the team can also upload PDFs. Requests from Materialspace dealers arrive already structured once that connection is built. Reading straight from the manufacturer's inbox, and connecting to their own systems, come later.

Corrections the quoting team makes are saved from the start, so the same problem doesn't have to be solved twice.

Phase 2 · Expected volume for planning

Weekly totals of expected orders against factory capacity. It depends on two things: enough requests coming through the product to make a useful forecast, and the manufacturer's answer about how they schedule production.

Phase 3 · Dealer activity on individual projects

Depends on what dealers say about sharing (section 07). It comes last because it's the one part that could damage trust with dealers.

The number that shows whether the strategy works

The other measures show whether the product works. This one shows whether the strategy does.

Say a manufacturer has 100 dealers, and a few already use Materialspace. Their requests arrive clean and get priced in minutes, while everyone else's arrive as PDFs. The hope is that the manufacturer starts telling its other dealers to send requests through Materialspace to get faster prices. Every dealer who joins that way is a new customer Materialspace didn't have to go out and win.

  • A few dealers a quarter: the manufacturer product is a useful thing to sell, but a modest one.
  • Dozens a quarter: each manufacturer brings a large group of dealers with it, and that's how the platform grows. Once dealers and manufacturers all work in the same place, it's also hard for a competitor to replace. Those are reasons two and three in your brief.

So this number tells Materialspace whether the manufacturer product is a side product or its main way to grow.

05Where I'd start, and why

Phase 0, with one manufacturer. Once requests start coming in, take them in whatever form they arrive, not only from Materialspace dealers.

  • The full product depends on it. Nothing gets priced automatically without prices.
  • It's the first reason in your brief. Only the manufacturer can confirm their catalog is accurate.
  • Only the manufacturer can agree to it, which makes it the natural first thing to ask for.
  • It's the cheapest thing to get wrong. There's little to build, and the answer is clear either way.
Why start with messy requests

An obvious question: Materialspace produces the dealer's estimate, so why read a printed, marked-up version of it?

Because today, most of a manufacturer's dealers don't use Materialspace. On day one, the product is only useful if it handles all of a manufacturer's requests, whatever form they come in. Once the manufacturer sees that requests sent through Materialspace are faster to price, it has a reason to move its other dealers onto it. That's how the dealer growth described above would happen.

06Competition

If a manufacturer doesn't use this, what do they use instead?

Keeping things as they are

Email, the manufacturer's own ordering portal (Smart has one), and people retyping orders. This is often the strongest competitor, because it costs nothing to keep.

2020 (now Design Flex, from Cyncly)

You've told me this is the main competitor today, and that customers would be glad to replace it with something faster and smarter. Dealers design kitchens in it, using catalogs from over 400 cabinet manufacturers.

What I take from that: customers dislike it but keep using it, most likely because the catalogs are all there. That supports the idea that a shared catalog network keeps customers. It also means being faster isn't enough on its own, because a replacement has to cover the catalogs dealers need.

2020 is built mainly for designing kitchens. From its website, I couldn't tell how much it does for quoting and ordering with manufacturers.

Quoting and ordering portals

KitchenDEV, Cabentry and Kitchen365 give manufacturers a branded portal where dealers configure, price and order. This kind of software is often called CPQ, short for configure, price, quote.

They expect dealers to come to the portal and enter orders there. KitchenDEV's product page, for example, doesn't mention reading orders sent by email. And each manufacturer has its own portal, so a dealer who buys from five manufacturers needs five logins. That's probably why orders still arrive by email.

General order-reading tools

Conexiom turns emailed orders, including PDFs, spreadsheets and handwritten notes, into orders in a manufacturer's system, for any industry. That's close to how the prototype handles messy requests, so reading messy documents isn't new on its own. What Conexiom doesn't have is knowledge of cabinets, such as how codes are built, finish rules and dealer discounts, or a network of dealers.

Parspec, the closest example

Parspec does something similar for electrical and plumbing products. It reads drawings and specifications, matches them to a catalog of millions of products, and produces quotes for distributors. It has raised $31 million. In July 2026 it launched a product for manufacturers' independent sales reps: requests come in, are turned into item lists, and quotes go out in minutes without retyping. That's very close to the prototype.

Three things stand out:

  • It built its catalog without manufacturers. It collects product information from about 4,000 manufacturer websites, and manufacturers don't use Parspec at all.
  • It serves sales reps rather than manufacturers. In electrical, reps do much of the quoting.
  • It charges per document, such as each quote produced. That's one possible pricing model.
Why cabinets need manufacturers as users, unlike Parspec

Parspec shows you can build a complete catalog without manufacturers. So why build for them? Two reasons specific to cabinets:

  • Cabinets are configured. A cabinet code is built from rules: type, width, height, depth and options (Smart spec book, page 70). You can't simply copy a list of products from a website.
  • Quotes get stuck at the manufacturer. In electrical, the rep or distributor produces the quote. In cabinets, anything non-standard waits for the manufacturer to price it. Smart's ordering guide says such items show no price "until we approve or correct the item" (page 73). The delay is at the manufacturer, so the manufacturer has to use the product.

One open question: do some cabinet manufacturers rely on independent reps to prepare quotes? The brief and Smart's documents both point to the manufacturer's own team. Where reps are involved, they could be a second group of users, or help bring dealers onto the platform.

Where the gap is

From public information, I didn't find anyone offering all three of these together:

  • built for pricing multifamily projects in volume, not single kitchens
  • accepts requests in whatever form they arrive
  • dealers and manufacturers on the same platform

Biggest threats, in order

  1. 2020 already has the catalogs and the dealers. If it adds automated quoting for multifamily projects, it starts well ahead.
  2. Parspec has the approach and the funding, and is already building for manufacturers' sales reps. It could move into cabinets.
  3. Keeping things as they are, if manufacturers are managing well enough today.
  4. The portals worry me least. They have no dealer network and, from what I could find, they don't read emailed orders.

Does sharing dealer activity with manufacturers hurt dealers?

This is an open question, and it decides how the pipeline feature should work.

Why it might matter. Discounts are negotiated partly on volume, so a manufacturer who can see all of a dealer's upcoming work knows more going into that conversation. And a manufacturer who can see which projects are live could try to sell directly to the builder and skip the dealer. As far as I know, some manufacturers already sell directly to large builders.

Why it might not. Discounts are often set in advance in volume tiers, and volume is a normal part of the conversation anyway. A quote request doesn't have to say who the builder is. And the manufacturer already receives every quote request today. The only new thing would be seeing whether a job is still active over time.

So it depends on what dealers say:

  • They don't mind: show dealer activity on every project. It's simpler, and more useful to the manufacturer.
  • They do mind: the manufacturer still sees the projects it was asked to quote, and weekly totals across all dealers, but not whether a dealer is still working on a particular job. That is shared only when a dealer asks for something in return, such as a firm ship date. Materialspace never asks dealers to share on a manufacturer's behalf, because even a request they turn down tells them their data is being discussed. Dealers are Materialspace's only paying customers today, and I don't want to put that at risk. The prototype's Pipeline screen shows this version.

If activity is only shared when a dealer asks for a date

There are two ways to design it, and I'd want to discuss them with you:

  • Simple: the dealer's activity on that job is shared as soon as they send the request. Easy to build. But if the manufacturer then can't commit to a date, the dealer shared information and got nothing back.
  • Held until the manufacturer commits: the manufacturer sees the request against its own capacity, and the dealer's activity is shared only if it commits to a date. Fairer to the dealer, but more complex: each request needs states (waiting, committed, expired, withdrawn) and a hold on the production slot for a set time.

Either way, two questions remain. First, what does "commit" mean when the dealer hasn't won the job yet? A manufacturer holding a production slot for a job the dealer might lose is keeping capacity free for nothing. Second, manufacturers could leave dates vague on purpose, so that dealers have to ask and share. One way to discourage that is to show dealers how often each manufacturer gives firm dates.

If I had to build it soon, I'd start with the simple version and track how often manufacturers can't commit.

Relying on Materialspace, from the manufacturer's side

A manufacturer joining the platform could worry about:

  • another company sitting between it and its dealers
  • its data being held somewhere else, and not being able to get it back out
  • prices going up once it's hard to leave
  • the platform helping dealers compare it with competitors, since Materialspace already lets dealers price jobs across several catalogs

Manufacturers already accept this with 2020, because that's where dealers are. What they'd want to avoid is a platform they can't leave that stops improving. I'd want to decide a few commitments early:

  • the manufacturer owns its catalog and quote data, and can export it
  • quotes go out under the manufacturer's name
  • no manufacturer can pay to be shown ahead of others
  • one manufacturer's data is never shown to another, for example which items dealers ask it for
  • the product works alongside the manufacturer's own systems instead of replacing them

There's also a longer-term effect. Once every manufacturer quotes the same day, speed stops being an advantage and becomes what everyone expects. Early manufacturers would join to get ahead, and later ones so they don't fall behind.

08What could go wrong

Product

Reading mistakes don't announce themselves. Codes like W1818 and W1858 both look valid, so a wrong reading can look correct. It might only come to light months later, as the wrong cabinet in a hundred-unit building. That's why the prototype shows how confident it is about each line, and doesn't recommend anything below a threshold.

The confidence threshold is a business decision. The 85% in the prototype is a placeholder. The real threshold should weigh the cost of accepting a wrong line against the cost of asking the dealer, and could differ by type of item.

Corrections need to be kept. Each fix the quoting team makes is an example of a hard case. If the product doesn't learn from them, the same problem has to be fixed again next time. I'd build this in from the start, because that's when there are the most corrections.

Process

The price book may be hard to use. The spec book is a print document, and the price book could be an old spreadsheet with years of in-house conventions.

The quoting team may not welcome it. It's often one or two people who have done it their way for years, and part of their value is that they're the only ones who know how.

Manufacturers may not want to do setup work early, especially if their catalog rarely changes.

Market

Dealers could see it as a threat, if they think it helps manufacturers go around them. They're Materialspace's paying customers, so getting this wrong affects the existing business, not just the new product.

2020 could add automated quoting, starting with the catalogs and dealers it already has.

Parspec could move into cabinets.

Sales reps could push back, where manufacturers rely on them.

09Assumptions and sources

Assumptions

  • The dollar amounts on the sample order are line totals, not prices per item.
  • The handwritten "F-L" means finished left: the left side of the cabinet gets a finished panel because it's exposed. "b" means "by".
  • Revenue being last in your list of reasons is deliberate, so early on this would be free or cheap for manufacturers.
  • Materialspace already reads manufacturer catalogs for its dealers.
  • What the dealer sends the manufacturer is a quote request. I've avoided "bid", which usually means what the dealer sends the builder.
  • The roles in the prototype (quoting, plant scheduling, sales operations) are my guess for a large manufacturer.
  • The $1.4 million example in section 01 uses made-up numbers.
  • The dealer names, volumes, and all numbers on the Catalog and Pipeline screens are made up.

What's taken from the documents you sent

  • The sample order document: all 16 line items, the crossed-out text, the handwritten changes, and the 8.875% sales tax.
  • The Smart Cabinetry spec book:
    • page 25: number of hinges by cabinet height, and depth changes in 3-inch steps
    • page 70: how cabinet codes are built
    • page 71: the order process, the 24-hour review, one finish per order, and materials bought 12 to 14 business days before building
    • page 72: dealers creating quotes in Smart's ordering system
    • page 73: the "Estimated Multiplier" field, and non-catalog items having no price until Smart approves them
    • page 74: 250 to 300 cabinets per truckload
