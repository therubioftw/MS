Prototype Approach

Building a product for manufacturers

A manufacturer prices every quote request the same day, whatever form it arrives in.

That's the product. The rest of this page covers what makes it possible, what it could lead to, and what I'd leave out for now.

This is the written version of the presentation: how I'd start, what I'd want to learn first, and who wins what. The prototype is here.

Where I landed. Today, a dealer using Materialspace finishes pricing a job and downloads an Excel file. What happens next depends on the manufacturer. Some want that file emailed as it is. Some have their own Excel template, so the dealer copies the SKUs into it first. Others want the whole job typed into their web portal.

At the manufacturer, someone then checks each SKU and its modifications, looks up prices in a separate price book, and applies the dealer's discount, which depends on the dealer's volume and on the size of the job. Anything that isn't a standard catalog item waits until someone approves and prices it.

A manufacturer product covers the other side of that exchange. Requests from Materialspace dealers arrive already structured and get priced in minutes. Other dealers still send Excel files or PDFs, and the product handles those too. The first step isn't reading catalogs, which Materialspace already does. It's getting the manufacturer to take charge of their own catalog data and connect their pricing.

The assumption everything rests on

Everything here assumes that quoting faster wins more jobs, by enough to matter to a manufacturer. The brief says so, and it's the first thing I'd check.

If manufacturers already quote within a couple of days, and dealers really choose on price or long relationships, then same-day quoting won't win much. The product would still be worth building, but it would be sold differently.

How I'd test it. Ask dealers, since they're the ones choosing. On your last few jobs, why did you go with that manufacturer? Would you rather have a firm price in two hours with one line still open, or a complete price in three days?

If speed isn't the reason. The same tool can be sold as a way to save work instead of a way to win jobs. Today someone at the manufacturer retypes and checks requests by hand. If the tool does most of that, the same team can handle more quotes without hiring. That's a pitch to whoever runs operations, not to the head of sales. Materialspace already makes this argument to dealers: a customer on your website describes doing with one estimator what used to take four. The catalog and pricing work stays first either way, because the rest depends on it.

01What the manufacturer is trying to get

Four goals, in the order I think a manufacturer would rank them. For each one I've noted which part of the prototype serves it, and where it only partly does.

1 · Get a price back before the other manufacturers quoting the same job

The brief puts speed to quote at the center of winning a job. How much it decides is the assumption above.

In the prototype: the Inbox shows every request in one list, however it arrived: sent from Materialspace, uploaded through the manufacturer's request link, or emailed. The request link is a simple page with the manufacturer's name on it, where a dealer who isn't on Materialspace uploads their Excel file (section 04). The quoting team can also add a request themselves by uploading an Excel file or PDF. Requests are sorted by how long the dealer has been waiting. On the Quote screen, SKUs and their modifications are priced automatically, with the dealer's standard discount and the manufacturer's job-size pricing. A custom line can go out with an estimated price, confirmed later, so it doesn't hold up the rest of the quote.

2 · Be the manufacturer that's easy to work with

Service is the other part of what the brief asks for.

In the prototype: questions for the dealer go out together with the price instead of delaying it. Catalog rules stay the manufacturer's rules. What changes is when the dealer finds out about them. For example, Smart allows one finish per order (spec book, page 71). If a request mixes two finishes, the quote flags it and suggests splitting the job into two orders, one per finish. The dealer learns that while pricing, instead of having the order rejected later.

Only partly covered. A lot of the friction comes later, at ordering. A multifamily project is quoted as one total, but it's ordered apartment by apartment, after shop drawings, by email or portal. Checking each of those orders against the approved quote is the next thing I'd build (Phase 2).

3 · Know what orders are coming before they arrive

In the brief, the manufacturer describes pricing a project, hearing nothing for up to a year, and then receiving a huge order the factory struggles to absorb.

This is harder than it looks. Materialspace doesn't know whether a quoted job was won. It only finds out when shop drawings are made, which is much later. And nothing about a dealer's project can be shared with a manufacturer without the dealer agreeing.

So dealers need a reason to share. I see two ways it could work, and section 07 covers both: a dealer shares a project and gets its price held until it orders, or a dealer shares a project and gets a firm delivery date.

In the prototype: the Pipeline screen shows expected volume by week against factory capacity, and projects that dealers have chosen to share.

4 · Keep the catalog accurate, with the manufacturer in charge of it

A catalog is more than a list of SKUs. It has rules, such as depth changes only in 3-inch steps (page 25), and real SKUs include modifications that the spec book doesn't show. As I understand it, Materialspace already reads manufacturer catalogs for its dealers and cleans up that data itself. With the manufacturer on the platform, they review and correct their own SKUs, rules and modifications, and bring in catalogs Materialspace doesn't have yet. Materialspace still does the technical work behind the scenes.

In the prototype: the Catalog screen shows how codes are read, which rules apply, where things are ambiguous, and what dealers ask for that isn't in the catalog. I don't know how often catalogs or prices change. They may stay the same for years. That's a question for manufacturers (section 03).

Not in scope for now

These are all things a manufacturer might reasonably want, and some may make sense later. I'd leave them out at the start, to focus on where the pain is biggest and where Materialspace can offer something new.

  • Helping manufacturers chase deals, by showing their sales teams which dealers are working on what. Nothing gets shared without the dealer agreeing, and that mechanism comes first.
  • Selling more to existing dealers, through upselling or promotions. Useful, but it doesn't make quoting faster.
  • Replacing the manufacturer's order or accounting system. That's a big, risky switch for them. The product should work alongside what they already use (section 07).
  • Charging manufacturers early. You've said distribution and a moat come first, and that this should be free or very cheap for manufacturers at the start. Later, it could be charged per quote, as Parspec does, or as a subscription.
  • Self-serve sign-up, and a second manufacturer. Both should wait until the first manufacturer shows the product works.

What faster quoting could be worth

I don't have real numbers, so this is a rough example to show how I'd think about it. I'd like your view on whether it's realistic.

  • A manufacturer prices 400 projects a year, averaging $180,000 each.
  • Answering the same day instead of in six days means winning 2 more out of every 100 projects priced.
  • That's 8 extra projects a year, or about $1.4 million in revenue.

Everything depends on that "2 more out of 100", and it's hard to measure. Dealers know why they chose a manufacturer, so I'd ask them. Is that a reasonable guess, or does speed decide more jobs than that, or fewer?

02How a job moves, and who's involved

From what you've described and Smart's order process, a multifamily job goes roughly like this:

  1. The dealer prices the job in Materialspace and downloads an Excel file.
  2. The dealer sends it to one or more manufacturers, each in its own way: the Excel file emailed as it is, the SKUs copied into the manufacturer's Excel template, or the job typed into the manufacturer's web portal.
  3. The manufacturer checks the SKUs and modifications, prices them, applies the dealer's discount and any job-size pricing, and sends a quote back.
  4. Nothing may happen for months, sometimes up to a year.
  5. If the job is won, shop drawings are made. These are detailed drawings of exactly which cabinets go where in each apartment, and the builder approves them before anything is ordered. The quote in step 3 doesn't need them.
  6. Orders go to the manufacturer apartment by apartment, by email or portal.
  7. At Smart, the dealer has 24 hours to review the order confirmation before it's locked (page 71).
  8. Materials are bought 12 to 14 business days before the cabinets are built (page 71), then production and delivery.

The prototype covers steps 2 and 3, and partly the planning problem between steps 3 and 6. Phase 2 would cover steps 6 and 7. I left production and delivery out on purpose.

At the manufacturer, I'd expect a few different people to be involved: the quoting team, who'd use it every day; whoever decides job-size pricing on large jobs, probably sales; whoever maintains the catalog and price book; and whoever schedules the plant. I don't know how often each of them would use it. A catalog might change once a year, while a busy plant's schedule changes every day. At a smaller manufacturer, the same two people might do all of it.

03What I'd want to learn before building

For each question, I've noted what the different answers would change. If I could only ask four, they'd be the ones marked Ask first.

From Materialspace's own data

How long after a quote do shop drawings usually appear, and how many quoted jobs never get them?

Weeks before the order: shop drawings are an early enough sign that orders are coming, at least for buying materials, if dealers agree to share it.

Days before the order: they come too late to help planning, so getting dealers to share projects earlier (section 07) matters more.

Which manufacturer do the most Materialspace dealers already buy from?

That's the natural first manufacturer, because bringing them on helps customers Materialspace already has.

How complete and reliable is the catalog data Materialspace already holds?

Mostly reliable: the first ask of the manufacturer is small, a review rather than a fix.

Many gaps or errors: the first step is a bigger cleanup, and the manufacturer needs a clearer reason to take it on.

From dealers

Mostly speed and responsiveness: the main assumption holds.

Mostly price or relationship: sell it as saving work rather than winning jobs, as described at the top.

How many manufacturers do you usually ask to price a job?

Several at once: it's a race, so speed matters most.

One preferred, others as backup: the relationship matters more, and speed less.

Two hours: sending prices with some lines estimated is worth building.

Firm date: start with the second option.

Neither is worth it: planning works only from orders already booked and totals, and it's a smaller benefit.

In documents or the price book: they can be loaded and checked against requests.

Rarely: it's set up once, with occasional updates, and maintenance is a small part of the product.

Often: keeping it current is real ongoing work, so having the manufacturer run it matters more. It also makes holding a price (section 07) more costly for them.

If manufacturers won't connect their prices

There's still a smaller product. Materialspace can turn any request into the manufacturer's own format, such as their Excel template or a file their portal accepts, with SKUs, modifications and catalog rules already checked. The manufacturer then prices it in their own system.

That removes most of the retyping and checking. It doesn't give same-day prices, so it falls short of the main goal, but it's a real starting point.

04How I'd break it into steps

I haven't put timelines on these. I'd estimate them with the engineering team, once we know how requests will reach the product and what it has to connect to.

Phase 0 · Show the manufacturer what Materialspace already knows

Start with one manufacturer. Show them their catalog as Materialspace already holds it, including the codes, rules and modifications it isn't sure about. Ask them to take over reviewing and correcting it, and to connect their price book and job-size pricing.

Two risks: they may not want to do any work this early, and there may not be much to maintain if their catalog rarely changes. That's why the first ask is a review rather than data entry, and why Materialspace does the heavy lifting.

Next step once prices are connected, or once we decide to start with the smaller product.
Phase 1 · Quote requests and problem lines

One manufacturer, taking requests in whatever form they arrive. This is most of the work, and where the main value is.

How requests get in:

  • From Materialspace dealers: sent straight to the manufacturer, with no Excel download.
  • From other dealers: a request link with the manufacturer's name on it, where the dealer uploads their Excel file or fills in the manufacturer's template. Plus a dedicated email address for requests sent as attachments.
  • From the quoting team: uploading an Excel file or PDF themselves, for requests that arrived some other way.

Each line is checked against the catalog rules, priced with its modifications and the dealer's discount, and flagged if something needs a person. Corrections the quoting team makes are saved from the start, so the same problem doesn't have to be solved twice.

Phase 2 · Orders checked against the quote

After shop drawings, orders arrive apartment by apartment. A 200-apartment project can mean 200 orders to check against one approved quote. The product matches each order to the quote, flags anything that changed, and sends it on in the format the manufacturer's ordering system already takes. It includes the dealer's 24-hour review (page 71).

It comes before planning because it doesn't need anyone to agree to share anything. The orders go to the manufacturer anyway, and you've said ordering has a lot of friction today.

Phase 3 · Planning

Expected volume by week against factory capacity, using three things: orders already booked in the manufacturer's own system, including ones that never went through Materialspace; quotes in progress; and projects dealers have chosen to share. It depends on dealers being willing to share, and on how the manufacturer schedules production.

The number that shows whether the strategy works

The other measures show whether the product works. This one shows whether the strategy does. You've said distribution comes first, so it's the number I'd lead with.

Say a manufacturer has 100 dealers, and a few already use Materialspace. Their requests arrive with one click and get priced in minutes. Everyone else sends an Excel file through the manufacturer's request link, and that works, but it's slower. The hope is that the manufacturer starts sending its other dealers the link, and some of those dealers see that Materialspace customers have it easier. Every dealer who joins that way is a new customer Materialspace didn't have to go out and win.

  • A few dealers a quarter: the manufacturer product is a useful thing to sell, but a modest one.
  • Dozens a quarter: each manufacturer brings a large group of dealers with it, and that's how the platform grows. Once dealers and manufacturers all work in the same place, it's also hard for a competitor to replace.

The catch: if the request link is too good, dealers get fast prices for free and have no reason to pay. So the link should do one thing, getting a request to the manufacturer, and none of the design and estimating that Materialspace customers pay for.

05Where I'd start, and why

Phase 0, with one manufacturer. Once requests start coming in, take them in whatever form they arrive, not only from Materialspace dealers.

  • The full product depends on it. Nothing gets priced automatically without prices.
  • It's the first reason in your brief. Only the manufacturer can confirm their catalog is accurate.
  • Only the manufacturer can agree to it, which makes it the natural first thing to ask for.
  • It's the cheapest thing to get wrong. There's little to build, and the answer is clear either way.
Why take requests from dealers who aren't on Materialspace

An obvious question: Materialspace already produces the dealer's request, so why handle Excel files and PDFs from other dealers?

Because today, most of a manufacturer's dealers don't use Materialspace. On day one, the product is only useful if it handles all of a manufacturer's requests. Most of those will probably be Excel files. Scanned, handwritten orders like the sample one are rare, but they happen, and the prototype shows one. Once the manufacturer sees that requests from Materialspace dealers are faster to price, it has a reason to move its other dealers onto it.

06Competition

If a manufacturer doesn't use this, what do they use instead?

Keeping things as they are

Dealers emailing Excel files or filled-in templates, or typing jobs into the manufacturer's portal (Smart has one), and people at the manufacturer retyping and checking by hand. This is often the strongest competitor, because it costs nothing to keep.

2020 (now Design Flex, from Cyncly)

You've told me this is the main competitor today, and that customers would be glad to replace it with something faster and smarter. Dealers design kitchens in it, using catalogs from over 400 cabinet manufacturers.

What I take from that: customers dislike it but keep using it, most likely because the catalogs are all there. That supports the idea that a shared catalog network keeps customers. It also means being faster isn't enough on its own, because a replacement has to cover the catalogs dealers need.

2020 is built mainly for designing kitchens. From its website, I couldn't tell how much it does for quoting and ordering with manufacturers.

Quoting and ordering portals

KitchenDEV, Cabentry and Kitchen365 give manufacturers a branded portal where dealers configure, price and order. This kind of software is often called CPQ, short for configure, price, quote.

They expect dealers to come to the portal and enter orders there. KitchenDEV's product page, for example, doesn't mention reading orders sent by email. And each manufacturer has its own process, so a dealer who buys from five manufacturers deals with five different ones. That matches what you described.

General order-reading tools

Conexiom turns emailed orders, including PDFs, spreadsheets and handwritten notes, into orders in a manufacturer's system, for any industry. So reading documents isn't new on its own. What Conexiom doesn't have is knowledge of cabinets, such as how codes are built, modifications, finish rules and dealer discounts, or a network of dealers.

Parspec, the closest example

Parspec does something similar for electrical and plumbing products. It reads drawings and specifications, matches them to a catalog of millions of products, and produces quotes for distributors. It has raised $31 million. In July 2026 it launched a product for manufacturers' independent sales reps: requests come in, are turned into item lists, and quotes go out in minutes without retyping. That's very close to the prototype.

Three things stand out:

  • It built its catalog without manufacturers. It collects product information from about 4,000 manufacturer websites, and manufacturers don't use Parspec at all.
  • It serves sales reps rather than manufacturers. In electrical, reps do much of the quoting.
  • It charges per document, such as each quote produced. That's one possible pricing model.
Why cabinets need manufacturers as users, unlike Parspec

Parspec shows you can build a complete catalog without manufacturers. So why build for them? Two reasons specific to cabinets:

  • Cabinets are configured. A cabinet code is built from rules: type, width, height, depth and options (Smart spec book, page 70), plus modifications that aren't in the spec book at all. You can't simply copy a list of products from a website.
  • Quotes get stuck at the manufacturer. In cabinets, the price depends on the manufacturer's price book, the dealer's discount and the size of the job, and anything non-standard waits for the manufacturer. Smart's ordering guide says such items show no price "until we approve or correct the item" (page 73). The delay is at the manufacturer, so the manufacturer has to use the product.

One open question: do some cabinet manufacturers rely on independent reps to prepare quotes? The brief and Smart's documents both point to the manufacturer's own team. Where reps are involved, they could be a second group of users, or help bring dealers onto the platform.

Where the gap is

From public information, I didn't find anyone offering all three of these together:

  • built for pricing multifamily projects in volume, not single kitchens
  • accepts requests in whatever form they arrive
  • dealers and manufacturers on the same platform

Biggest threats, in order

  1. 2020 already has the catalogs and the dealers. If it adds automated quoting for multifamily projects, it starts well ahead.
  2. Parspec has the approach and the funding, and is already building for manufacturers' sales reps. It could move into cabinets.
  3. Keeping things as they are, if manufacturers are managing well enough today.
  4. The portals worry me least. They have no dealer network and, from what I could find, they don't read emailed orders.

The first option is similar to something called deal registration in IT and networking sales, where a reseller registers a deal with a vendor and gets better pricing on it. The second one is closer to how dealers already talk to manufacturers.

Either way, Materialspace never asks dealers to share on a manufacturer's behalf. Dealers are its only paying customers today, and I don't want to put that at risk. If dealers don't want either, planning works from orders already booked and weekly totals only.

Relying on Materialspace, from the manufacturer's side

A manufacturer joining the platform could worry about:

  • another company sitting between it and its dealers
  • its data being held somewhere else, and not being able to get it back out
  • prices going up once it's hard to leave
  • the platform making it easy for dealers to compare it with competitors

Manufacturers already accept this with 2020, because that's where dealers are. What they'd want to avoid is a platform they can't leave that stops improving. I'd want to decide a few commitments early:

  • the manufacturer owns its catalog and quote data, and can export it
  • quotes go out under the manufacturer's name
  • no manufacturer can pay to be shown ahead of others
  • one manufacturer's data is never shown to another, for example which items dealers ask it for
  • it works with the systems the manufacturer already has:
    • Price book: read from wherever it lives today, such as an Excel file, not re-entered.
    • Order entry: checked orders go into their ordering system or portal in the format it already takes.
    • Production schedule: expected volume is fed into however they plan today. The product doesn't schedule the plant.

There's also a longer-term effect. Once every manufacturer quotes the same day, speed stops being an advantage and becomes what everyone expects. Early manufacturers would join to get ahead, and later ones so they don't fall behind.

08What could go wrong

Product

Reading mistakes don't announce themselves. Two codes can both look valid, so a wrong reading can look correct. It might only come to light months later, as the wrong cabinet in a hundred apartments. That's why the prototype shows how confident it is about each line, and doesn't recommend anything below a threshold.

The confidence threshold is a business decision. The 85% in the prototype is a placeholder. The real threshold should weigh the cost of accepting a wrong line against the cost of asking the dealer, and could differ by type of item.

Modifications and rules may be hard to capture. You've said real SKUs include modifications the spec book doesn't show. If those rules only live in the quoting team's heads, loading them is slow, and the product misprices lines until it's done.

Corrections need to be kept. Each fix the quoting team makes is an example of a hard case. If the product doesn't learn from them, the same problem has to be fixed again next time. I'd build this in from the start, because that's when there are the most corrections.

Process

The price book may be hard to use. You've said it's a list of SKUs and prices. It may still carry years of in-house conventions that only the quoting team understands.

The quoting team may not welcome it. Part of their value is that they're the ones who know how pricing really works.

Job-size pricing may need a person. If someone has to approve every large quote, the delay moves from the quoting team to them.

Manufacturers may not want to do setup work early, especially if their catalog rarely changes.

Market

Dealers could see it as a threat, if they think it gives manufacturers too much insight into their business. They're Materialspace's paying customers, so getting this wrong affects the existing business, not just the new product.

The request link could replace paying for Materialspace, if it does too much for free (section 04).

2020 could add automated quoting, starting with the catalogs and dealers it already has.

Parspec could move into cabinets.

Sales reps could push back, where manufacturers rely on them.
